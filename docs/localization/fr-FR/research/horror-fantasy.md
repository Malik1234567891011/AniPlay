# French horror and fantasy register

## PART 1 — HORROR

### The governing concept: *le fantastique*, not "horror"

French genre tradition organises around **le fantastique**, defined by Todorov
as *"l'hésitation qu'il produit entre le surnaturel et le naturel"*. The engine
is **doubt, not display**: realism as the platform, rational ambiguity, the
protagonist reacting with *"refus, rejet ou peur"*.

**The corollary is your most important DON'T.** French has a native pejorative
for over-the-top horror: **`grand-guignolesque`**, designating works *"abusant
de la violence ou d'effets grandiloquents."* English has no equally sharp word.
**Overwritten French horror does not merely fall flat — it lands in a named
category of ridicule.**

### The cliché blacklist (from French editors' own published lists)

`un frisson parcourut son échine` — **a 19th-century commonplace**, listed by Le
Robert as a fixed collocation, running through Dumas and the French Poe. It will
not read as wrong French; it will read as **stock, generic, translated-from-
nothing**.

Also blacklisted: `avoir peur au ventre` · `froid dans le dos` ·
`horreur indicible` · `paralysé d'effroi` · `silence abyssal` ·
`silence pesant` · `noir complet` · `les ténèbres vous enveloppent` ·
`sueur froide` · `bain de sang` · `son cœur battait la chamade` ·
`la forêt était sombre` · `visage ravagé par la souffrance` ·
`des abîmes insondables` · `des plaies béantes`.

**Writing tics** (distinct from clichés): `effectivement`, `en effet`,
`manifestement`, `cela dit`, `en fait`, `un peu`. **`du coup` is flagged by
French editors as an outright faute de langue, not just a tic.** The key
framing: tics *"ne sont pas fautifs en soi"* — the problem is
**`leur redondance`**.

Note that oral fillers behave differently on the page: what sounds natural
spoken **`pique les yeux à l'écrit`**. Dose them; don't transcribe.

### The model text: Maupassant, *Le Horla*

> *"je sens aussi que quelqu'un s'approche de moi, me regarde, me palpe, monte
> sur mon lit, s'agenouille sur ma poitrine, me prend le cou entre ses mains et
> serre… serre…"*
>
> *"Vers dix heures, je monte dans ma chambre. À peine entré, je donne deux
> tours de clef, et je pousse les verrous ; j'ai peur… de quoi ?"*

What he does, all directly portable:
1. **Present tense, first person, diary form** — canonical French literature,
   not an anglicism. Precedent for a second-person interactive app.
2. **Concrete verb chains, not adjectives.** Seven plain verbs, zero words
   meaning "terrifying".
3. **Self-interrogation**: `j'ai peur… de quoi ?` does more than any `indicible`.
4. **Points de suspension and repetition as the tension device** —
   `serre… serre…`. **The single most transferable technique.** French uses this
   where English horror uses the hard fragment.
5. **Banal precision anchors the dread**: `Vers dix heures`, `deux tours de clef`.

### The Lovecraft translation debate — the key register case study

**Jacques Papy** (the reference French Lovecraft for decades) is now the
cautionary example. He shortened Lovecraft's long sentences and cut. But the
more instructive charge is **added register**:

- **Camus:** *"La chose la plus miséricordieuse qui soit au monde est bien, je
  crois, l'incapacité de l'esprit humain…"*
- **Papy:** *"À mon sens, la plus grande faveur que le **Ciel** nous ait
  accordée…"*

**Papy inserted `le Ciel`** — a religious agent absent from the English. That is
exactly the failure mode of "elevated" horror French: *the translator adds a
metaphysics the author didn't write, because it sounds more literary.*

**David Camus's method is the best single principle found:** he asks throughout
*"comment Lovecraft aurait écrit cela en français"*. The result is praised as
***"fidèle sans artifice, précis sans excès de sophistication."***
**Put that phrase in the style guide.**

(François Bon argues the opposite — that Lovecraft's deliberately 18th-century
syntax justifies reaching for Saint-Simon. **Take the Camus line, not the Bon
line**: archaic French horror register is defensible only when the source
author's own syntax is period-marked and deliberate. For a modern mobile app it
is not.)

### Mechanics

**Tense.** `passé simple` is written/literary only and produces *"un récit plus
classique et distancié"*. `passé composé` is spoken/first-person.
**`présent de narration`** *"donne l'impression que l'action se déroule sous les
yeux du lecteur, renforçant l'immersion."*

**Recommendation: présent de narration as the default narrative tense.** It is
the documented French tool for immersion, it is what *Le Horla*'s diary does,
and **it sidesteps the passé simple trap entirely**. Reserve passé simple for
in-world artifacts — chronicles, legends, grimoires — where it reads as
deliberate diegetic register rather than translator affectation.

**Sentence fragments — yes, French has native names for this.**
**`le style coupé`** (short independent propositions, *"un rythme rapide et
vif"*) and **`la phrase affective`** (*"désorganisée par l'émotion. Rapide,
hachée"*) plus **`phrases nominales`**. French stylistics explicitly sanctions
fragmented, verbless, emotion-driven prose. Also adopt this rule wholesale:
***"ce qu'il y a de plus important à dire doit être placé en dernier"*** — put
the payload at the end. For a jump-scare beat, that is the whole technique.

**But the failure mode is documented.** Jean Sola on Martin's most famous
fragment sequence:

> **Martin:** *"The right eye was open. The pupil burned blue. It saw."*
> **Sola:** *"L'oeil droit, grand ouvert, voyait, lui. Car la pupille en
> flamboyait d'une flamme bleue."*

Three hammer-blows fused into two baroque sentences, an added causal `Car`, an
added emphatic `lui`, the tautology `flamboyait d'une flamme`. Across one
passage **Martin's seven short sentences become four**. He also **added
punctuation absent from the source** — one added exclamation mark turning a
character from coldly measured to unnervingly cheerful.

**Conclusion: resist the connector reflex.** The traditional French translator's
instinct is to smooth, subordinate and connect — inserting `car`, `alors que`,
`tandis que` — and that instinct is now criticised in French as a fault. Allow
fragments, but let French bear one or two per beat alongside its own tension
punctuation (points de suspension), not a whole paragraph of English-shaped
hard fragments.

**Onomatopoeia: do not spell the sound.** French acknowledges *"la verbalisation
est plus problématique que dans d'autres langues"*; its inventory (`Crac`,
`Paf`, `Boum`) is comic-book-coded. **Name it with a precise noun instead** —
`un craquement`, `un grincement`, `un raclement`, `un froissement`,
`un chuintement`, `un claquement`, `un cliquetis`. `Le plancher craque` beats
`CRAC !` every time.

### Horror DO / DON'T

**DON'T:** the cliché list above · `indicible`/`innommable`/`insoutenable` as
atmosphere adjectives · `soudain`/`tout à coup` as a beat-opener ·
`étrangement`/`bizarrement` and -ment adverbs generally (French MT's
fingerprint) · `sinistre`/`glacial`/`lugubre` as default modifiers ·
`les ténèbres` · **adding a metaphysical register the source lacks** ·
**fusing short beats with `car`/`tandis que`** · **adding exclamation marks** ·
piling on violence (→ `grand-guignolesque`) · `du coup` · spelled-out onomatopoeia.

**DO:** présent de narration · verb chains over adjectives ·
**points de suspension as the tension unit** · narrator self-interrogation ·
banal concrete anchors (numbers, household objects) · precise sound nouns ·
**non-visual senses** (not `la forêt était sombre` but
*`l'air sentait la mousse et le bois pourri`*) · phrase nominale + style coupé in
moderation · payload last · ordinary character overwhelmed (Jean Ray's
*"personnages souvent falots ou insignifiants"*) · folk/oral specificity
(Seignolle) · **read aloud in QA** — every French source names this as the
cliché detector.

**The three cliché-repair moves**, as a rewrite protocol: **`décaler`** (shift),
**`substituer`** (swap a word so it re-anchors in your world), **`renverser`**
(invert its expected sense). Note this is *not* "delete all clichés" — the
French position is that good writers **renew** them.

## PART 2 — FANTASY

### Is pseudo-medieval French normal, or ridiculous?

**It is a live tradition, but the professional consensus has moved decisively
against it, and the flagship attempt is the most-mocked translation in French
fantasy.**

A peer-reviewed comparison of Sola, Marcel, Ledoux and Lauzon finds:
- **Lexical archaism transfers fine** (`greaves`→`jambières`, `gorgerin`), though
  both translators **over-embellish** — a plain `long sword` becoming `flamberge`
  imports a 16th-century German aesthetic.
- **Grammatical archaism does not transfer.** Both Tolkien translators
  **systematically abandon** the archaic inversions, because
  *"une couronne d'acier il portait"* reads as **pastiche, not literariness**.
- Translators prefer ***"l'invisibilisation à l'exposition"***, attributed to
  **French aesthetic conventions that reject pastiche**. Sola's method is
  labelled with Berman's pejorative term ***"l'ennoblissement"***. Concrete
  instance: Martin's plain `colorful as a rainbow` becomes
  ***`diaprures et bigarrures`***.

### The Sola/Marcel *Trône de fer* controversy

Wikipedia's summary: *"style archaïsant assez éloigné de celui, plus simple, de
Martin et… conservation de certains noms propres en anglais tandis que d'autres
ont été traduits."* Two charges: **archaising style** and **inconsistent naming**.

**Archaic vocabulary nobody uses.** Readers mock the vocabulary explosion:
***"Combien de termes pour désigner une épée ! Pertuisane, rapière, estramaçon,
braquemart"***. Reports of words *"tellement archaïsants… que certains
n'apparaissent même dans aucun dictionnaire"*. Also `farauds` (**an adjective
with no counterpart in the English at all**), `jouvenceau`, `derechef`.

**Ambient invention.** `Slaver's Bay` → **`Baie des Serfs`** (a European-feudal
concept over Martin's antique slavery); a `slim tower` → **`minaret`**; a
`sandal` → **`babouche`**; and `froid polaire`, invoking **Earth's poles inside
a secondary world**.

**Errors produced *by* the archaising reflex.** `past fifty` →
`depuis cinquante ans et plus` (breaking a character's timeline);
`He was a bastard` → `le sentiment de sa bâtardise`.

**Terminology the author himself objected to.** Sola rendered **`direwolf` as
`loup-garou`** — *werewolf*. **George R. R. Martin publicly criticised this.**
Also `kraken` → `seiche` (cuttlefish). Marcel later corrected to `loup géant`.

**The naming policy was the #1 structural complaint:** keep `Winterfell`,
`Littlefinger`, `Hightower` in English while francising `Smallwood` and `Umber`.
The result is **tonal confusion**.

**But much of what he did translate is genuinely good** and survived:
`King's Landing`→**`Port-Réal`** · `Riverrun`→**`Vivesaigues`** ·
`Storm's End`→**`Accalmie`** · `Oldtown`→**`Villevieille`** ·
`Dragonstone`→**`Peyredragon`** · `Highgarden`→**`Hautjardin`** ·
`Sunspear`→**`Lancehélion`** · `Oathkeeper`→**`Féale`**.
**The name inventions largely survived the controversy; the register did not.**

**Marcel's own diagnosis** is the key sentence: ***"George R. R. Martin a un
style très simple pour montrer le côté présent des personnages"*** — Martin
writes plainly *deliberately*, so **Sola's medievalising didn't just add
flavour, it defeated the author's actual technique.** Marcel's approach:
***"un style plus direct, moins médiévalisant."***

### The Ledoux/Lauzon *Seigneur des anneaux* retranslation

Ledoux (1972) won a prize but his version *"est truffée de coquilles et
d'erreurs"* — structurally, he had **no access to *The Silmarillion*** or to
Tolkien's translator guidance. Canonical error: **`the Valar` → `le Valar`**.

Lauzon (2014–16) worked from Tolkien's **1975 *Nomenclature***.
`Baggins` Sacquet→**Bessac** · `Treebeard` Sylvebarbe→**Barbebois** ·
`Shadowfax` Gripoil→**Scadufax** · `Rivendell` Fondcombe→**Fendeval** ·
`Rangers` Rôdeurs→**Coureurs** · `Bamfurlong`→**Faverolle** ·
`Lake-town` Lacville→**Le Bourg-du-Lac**.

**His principles, and they are directly applicable:**
- **On tu/vous** — he *refused* to flatten it: having all Hobbits tutoyer each
  other *"aurait été s'arroger des prérogatives d'auteur"*. He differentiates
  **Frodo's refined register vs Sam's popular register**.
- **Vouvoiement as a relationship signal, not archaic decoration:**
  **Gandalf vouvoie Frodo** (respectful distance); **Galadriel tutoie him** — to
  mark *"cette compréhension immédiate qu'elle a de lui."* **This is the best
  model in French fantasy for handling T/V, and it maps directly onto
  interactive fiction with relationship state.**
- **Method:** *"en partant de la source"*, not by revising Ledoux. `Bamfurlong`
  (bean field) → **`Faverolle`**, following genuine French topographic naming
  rather than inventing a fantasy-sounding word.
- Avoids **simplifying or flattening Tolkien's variety of registers**.
- Translated verse **as verse**, and was praised for it where Ledoux was
  criticised for translating literally.

**Reception:** praise is about *readability and music* — *"vraiment plus facile
à lire, vraiment plus digeste"*, *"un récit fluide, poétique et presque
chantant"*. Criticism is nostalgia, film-name divergence, and mockery of
specific coinages (*"Frodo Bessac"* said to sound like a sweet-wine producer;
*"Fendeval"* like a D&D campaign).

### The two controversies point the same direction

Sola was **punished for archaising**; Lauzon **rewarded for de-archaising** and
prioritising fluidity, music and register-differentiation-by-character. Two
independent French fandoms, two decades apart, same verdict:
**plain, fluid, rhythmically faithful French wins; grandiloquent archaic French
loses.**

**But "plain" ≠ "flat."** Lauzon's win was not simplification — his gains were
*rhythm, verse, and per-character register*.

### Conventions for invented terms

**Capitalisation — where MT-flavoured French is most visible.** French
capitalises far less than English. `le mont Blanc` — the generic term stays
lowercase, the specifier takes the capital. So `la montagne Solitaire`,
`la baie des Serfs`, `le mur de Glace`.
- Titles/functions lowercase in generic use: `le roi`, `la reine`,
  `le capitaine de la garde`. Uppercase only when uniquely substituting for a
  name.
- Peoples capitalised as nouns, lowercase as adjectives: **`les Elfes`** but
  **`un artisan elfe`**, `la langue elfique`. Getting this wrong is a dead
  giveaway.
- Institutions capitalise, buildings don't: `l'Ordre de la Flamme` vs
  `l'ordre monastique local`.
- **Deified abstractions take a capital** (`la Fortune`) — your licence for
  `la Mort`, `les Ténèbres`, `l'Ombre` as personified forces.
- English fantasy Title-Cases everything (*the Iron Throne*). French does not —
  `le Trône de fer`, lowercase `fer`. **A French page full of Title Case is the
  most instantly recognisable machine-translation tell there is. Make it a lint
  rule.**

**Hyphenation — why `Port-Réal` feels natively French.** French compound proper
nouns hyphenate all elements except the initial article: `la rue du
Faubourg-Saint-Honoré`, `le lycée Louis-le-Grand`, `les États-Unis`. So
`Port-Réal`, `Vif-Argent`, `Le Bourg-du-Lac` are **French toponymy operating
normally**, and Lauzon's `Le Bourg-du-Lac` is more idiomatic than Ledoux's
`Lacville` precisely *because* it hyphenates like a real commune. **Use
hyphenated compounds freely — the strongest single signal of natively-conceived
French.**

**Gender for invented nouns — honest finding: there is no codified rule.** It is
translator discretion. Working heuristics: ending-driven analogy
(*-e/-ie/-ure/-elle/-esse* → feminine; consonant/*-on/-eur/-ard/-in* →
masculine); semantic anchoring to an existing French noun; borrowed names
default masculine. **Whatever you choose, freeze it in the glossary before
writing** — French gender propagates into every article, adjective and participle
in every branch. A gender flip mid-corpus is a rewrite, not a find-and-replace.
**Treat gender as a first-class glossary field.**

**Translate or transliterate?** French strongly prefers to translate meaningful
names and coin French forms (`Uzumaki`→`Spirale`, `witcher`→`sorceleur`,
`King's Landing`→`Port-Réal`). **But consistency beats the choice itself** —
Sola's crime was doing it **arbitrarily**.

Also worth knowing: French chose **`Donjons et Dragons`** even though the
accurate translation of that sense of *dungeon* is `oubliette` (a French
`donjon` is a keep — the opposite of an underground cell). **In French genre
localisation, sound and memorability legitimately outrank literal accuracy for
headline proper nouns.**

### Fantasy DO / DON'T

**DON'T:** archaic inversions · systematic passé simple in real-time narration ·
obsolete lexis for its own sake (`derechef`, `jouvenceau`, `estramaçon`) ·
**adding anything** · ennoblissement · English Title Case ·
real-world-Earth references inside a secondary world · mixed naming policy ·
drifting `tu`/`vous` · fantasy-phonetic coinages that ignore French morphology.

**DO:** plain fluid modern French · flavour in **nouns and names, not syntax** ·
hyphenated toponym compounds · coin from real French morphology · sound over
literalism for headline terms · **differentiate register by character** ·
**use T/V as an authored relationship signal** · translate verse as verse ·
French capitalisation discipline · personify with a capital · passé simple only
for in-world lore texts · **freeze the glossary before translating any prose**.

## QA rules derived from this research

1. **Sentence-count parity** on horror beats — 3 source sentences must not
   become 1 (the Sola `It saw.` test)
2. **Added-connector lint** — flag `car`, `en effet`, `tandis que`, `alors que`
   where the source has a full stop
3. **Added-punctuation lint** — flag `!` in FR where EN has `.`
4. **Cliché blocklist** as a hard fail
5. **-ment adverb density cap** per screen
6. **Title Case lint** — flag any invented term with a capitalised non-initial
   common noun
7. **T/V consistency check** per character pair across all branches
8. **Glossary gender-agreement check**
9. **Read-aloud pass**
10. **`du coup` = automatic fail**
