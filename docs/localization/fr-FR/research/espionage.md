# French espionage / thriller vocabulary — Window Seven register

Primary source is the **DGSE's own published lexicon**, the *Dico d'espions*
(94-page official PDF, dgse.gouv.fr), plus fr.wikipedia's *Glossaire du
renseignement*, Wiktionnaire, and attested French press headlines.

## The single most important finding

**In official French intelligence usage, `un agent` is NOT the spy protagonist —
it is the recruited foreign source.** The DGSE states it flatly:

> « Un agent est un individu qui accepte sciemment et avec contrepartie de
> fournir des informations secrètes ou un soutien clandestin à un officier
> traitant. »
> « En ce sens, contrairement à ce que l'on croit, l'agent n'est pas membre d'un
> service de renseignement, mais une source. »

So « Tu es notre meilleur agent » reads, to a French player who knows the
domain, as *"you are our best informant."* The insider words for the player
character are **officier traitant**, **officier de renseignement**, or
**clandestin**.

**The catch, and the opportunity:** journalistic and popular French *does* use
`agent` loosely — « agent de la DGSE », « agent secret » are everywhere in the
press. **Exploit the split: let civilians and in-game news broadcasts say
`agent`, and let the service itself say `officier traitant` / `OT` /
`clandestin`.** That contrast alone will make the French read authentic.

## Surveillance

**`la surveillance`** is correct but *unmarked* — what a bureaucrat or a CCTV
sign says. Genre French picks something specific. Useful officialese:
**`un dispositif`** = the whole deployed apparatus. « Un dispositif est en
place. » No clean English equivalent.

**STAKEOUT → `une planque`** (f.), argot policier, now mainstream.
`être en planque` (default) · `faire une planque` · `monter une planque` ·
`planquer` · `lever la planque` · `se planquer` (reflexive, different).
**Bonus: `un soum`** — police argot for the unmarked surveillance vehicle.
**`planque` also = safe house** (see below).
**Trap:** not `une surveillance`, not `un guet` (that is a kid watching for the
teacher).

**TAIL → `une filature`** (f.), verb **`filer`**. DGSE-defined, and rare in
being simultaneously **official DGSE vocabulary, police argot and journalistic
French** — extremely safe everywhere.
`prendre quelqu'un en filature` · `filer quelqu'un` · `être filé` ·
**`semer`** = to shake a tail (not `perdre`) · `une rupture de filature` = the
watchers' word for losing the target · `décrocher` = to break off deliberately.
Counter-technique: **`l'itinéraire de sécurité`** — the French SDR. No common
abbreviation; spell it out.

## Technical collection

**WIRETAP → `les écoutes`** (f. pl.) — **plural by default**. `mettre sur
écoute` · `être sur écoute` · `une table d'écoute` (Cold War flavour).
Legal distinction worth using: **`écoutes judiciaires`** (magistrate) vs
**`écoutes administratives`** / **`interceptions de sécurité`** (executive).
**Trap:** « écouter quelqu'un » alone means to listen to them speak.

From the 2015 *loi renseignement*: **`les techniques de renseignement`** (the
umbrella legal term a French bureaucrat uses) · `le recueil de données de
connexion` · **`l'IMSI-catcher`** (English kept) · `les boîtes noires` ·
**`la sonorisation`** = bugging a room, a real French word with no snappy
English equivalent · `la captation d'images` · `les balises` /
`baliser un véhicule` · `la CNCTR` (oversight body).

**INTEL → there is no French "intel."**
`le renseignement` (uncountable — the discipline) · `un renseignement` (a piece)
· `les infos` (what a field officer says to a source) ·
`un bulletin de renseignement` (**`BR`**) · **`une note`** (the everyday word for
the written product) · **`une note blanche`** (unsourced, unsigned domestic memo
— loaded, very French).
**Never « de l'intel ».** Note `les renseignements` can echo the disbanded
**Renseignements généraux (RG)**.

**CHATTER → no French equivalent; do not calque.** « Les bavardages » reads as
translated. Use `le trafic` / `le volume des communications` ·
**« ça parle beaucoup »** / « ça s'agite » (best for dialogue) ·
**`des remontées`** — « On a des remontées inquiétantes » · `des signaux faibles`.

**Collection sources — the `RO-` family.** France re-abbreviates the INT family:
**`ROHUM`** (HUMINT) · **`ROEM`** (SIGINT) · **`ROIG`** (GEOINT) ·
**`ROSO`** (OSINT). But the English acronyms are **also** widely used in France.
**A DGSE officer says `ROHUM`; a journalist says `HUMINT`; a cyber contractor
says `OSINT`.** Free authenticity lever.
**`un capteur`** = "sensor", the umbrella word for any means of collection.
English has no everyday equivalent; French uses it constantly.

## People

**INFORMANT.** `un indic` (argot policier, **péjoratif**) · `un informateur`
(official police) · `un aviseur` (customs specifically) · `une source` (what an
intelligence service says — **never `indic` inside a service**).
Criminal-milieu insults: **`une balance`** (f., common, harsh) · `un mouchard` ·
`un mouton` (cell-mate informant) · `un rat` · `un doulos` (dated,
Melville-flavoured) · `un délateur` (formal, WWII resonance). Verb:
`balancer quelqu'un`.
**Gender trap:** `une balance` and `une taupe` are feminine regardless of sex.

**SOURCE → `une source`** (f.). The DGSE distinguishes **`une source
inconsciente`** (unwitting — being milked without knowing) from **`un agent`**
(formally recruited, witting, compensated). **A branching narrative can
dramatise the moment one becomes the other. English has no comparable pair.**
`traiter une source` · `recruter` · `griller` · **`une source mise en sommeil`**.

**ASSET → no French word. The biggest lexical gap.** French Wikipedia has no
article; the interlanguage link is empty. Use `une source` / `un agent` for a
person you run; **`un relais`**, `un correspondant`, `un point d'appui` for
someone in place; **`des moyens`** for capability — « On n'a plus de moyens sur
zone. » **Never `un actif`** (financial) or **`un atout`** (reads like a
dictionary lookup).

**MOLE → `une taupe`** (f.) — **always feminine, even for a man.**
« **La taupe, c'est lui.** » is correct, idiomatic and dramatically excellent.
Official French collapses it into `agent double`; journalism and fiction keep
them distinct, as English does.

**HANDLER → `un officier traitant`**, short **`le traitant`** or **`l'OT`**.
The most authentic term available; it appears on the DGSE's own recruitment
pages. Verb family is the payoff: **`traiter`** — « C'est moi qui le traite. » ·
`la traitance` · `le traitement` (approach → manipulation → recruitment) ·
**`la manipulation`** (official and **not pejorative** here).
**Serious trap: do not translate handler as `le manipulateur`** (that means a
manipulative creep). **Avoid `le contrôleur`** — that is Canadian usage and
reads as a Québécois calque.
Adjacent and very *Bureau des Légendes*: **`la veilleuse`/`le veilleur`** — the
desk officer watching over a clandestine in real time. No clean English name.

**HONORABLE CORRESPONDANT** (m.), **`HC`** — « accepte consciemment et **sans
contrepartie** de fournir du renseignement ». **The `agent` (paid) vs `HC`
(unpaid, patriotic) distinction is doctrinally load-bearing and uniquely
French.** Typical HCs are French nationals in big firms abroad. Below it sits
`un contact utile`. A French reader who knows the domain will register `HC` as a
sign you did your homework.

**CONTACT.** `un contact` (the person) · `un rendez-vous` / `un RV` / `un RDV` ·
**`un rencard`** (familiar — « On a rencard à 18h. »; also means a date, useful
ambiguity) · `une rencontre`.
**Recruitment verb: `tamponner`** — a "bump" in English tradecraft, so named
because it often starts by gently bumping a vehicle. `un tamponnage`.
**`faire un environnement`** — DGSE: research to know a target precisely before
approach. English would say "work up a target package." Beautifully
untranslatable.

**CUTOUT → `un coupe-circuit`** (m.), confirmed with its own French Wikipedia
article. The tool of **`le cloisonnement des réseaux`**. Use `le cloisonnement`
liberally — it pairs with the DGSE's **`besoin d'en connaître`**, and
« **Vous n'avez pas le besoin d'en connaître.** » is a perfect, chilling,
authentic line.

## Identity

**COVER → `la couverture`** (f.). Three doctrinal types: `couverture légère` ·
`couverture diplomatique` (blown → merely `persona non grata`) ·
`couverture non officielle` (NOC; Russian `illégaux`).
`sous couverture` (no article) · `griller`/`brûler sa couverture` ·
`se faire griller` · `tenir sa couverture`.
**Trap:** French **burns** things, it does not blow them — avoid « soufflée ».

**LEGEND → `une légende`** (f.), and the DGSE definition is **narrower than
English "backstory"**: the *officer's* fictitious personal or professional
situation. `sous légende` (no article) · `créer une légende` ·
`une légende qui tient` · `une identité fictive` (**`IF`**) — the paperwork,
distinct from the biography.
Related: **`un clandestin`** / colloquially **`un clandé`** — the officer living
under legend abroad · **`la clandestinité`** (carries Resistance resonance) ·
**`un pseudonyme`** — the service word for a codename (press and fiction say
`nom de code`).

**TARGET → `une cible`** (f.) · **`un objectif`** (the service's word;
« traiter l'objectif » where English says "service the target") ·
`le ciblage`. Colloquially « **notre homme** » / « **notre bonhomme** » —
genuinely how French operators refer to a male target, contemptuous-affectionate.

## Operations

`une opération clandestine` · `une opération secrète` · **`une opé`** (familiar,
military — « On monte une opé. »; verb is `monter`) · `un dispositif` ·
`une mission`.

**DGSE Service Action vocabulary, and it is spectacular:**
`le service Action` (SA) · **`les opérations « homo »`** (neutralisation, from
*homicide* — in scare quotes even on Wikipedia) · **`les opérations « arma »`**
(sabotage) · **`l'entrave`** — the operative French word for the whole family of
"make it not happen" actions; **English has no single noun** ·
`la troisième option`.

**CELL → `une cellule`**; sleeper cell **`une cellule dormante`** (often in
scare quotes in the French press — journalists treat it as slightly
sensational). Note `une cellule` in officialese often means a task force
(`la cellule de crise`).

**SLEEPER AGENT → `un agent dormant`**, but the **idiomatic verb phrase is
better**: `mettre en sommeil` · `réveiller` / `activer` — « On le réveille. » ·
`un réseau en sommeil`. **Trap: `un agent endormi` is wrong** (literally asleep).

**SAFE HOUSE → `une planque`. There is no other word.** French Wikipedia has no
article — not a lexicalised French concept. Alternatives: `un lieu sûr`
(formal) · **`une base arrière`** (a group's logistical hub) · **`une cache`**
(for *objects*, not people — distinguish carefully) · `un appartement nourrice`
(drug-trade argot).
**Trap: « une maison sûre » is a calque and reads as bad translation.**

**DEAD DROP → `une boîte aux lettres morte`**, abbreviated **`BLM`**.
DGSE-defined. `charger une BLM` · **`relever une BLM`** (`relever` is the right
verb, as for a letterbox) · `une BLM grillée`. In dialogue characters would say
« la BLM » or « la boîte » once established.
**Trap:** « boîte morte » alone is wrong.

**EXTRACTION → `l'exfiltration`** (f.), verb **`exfiltrer`** — fully naturalised.
Distinguish: `exfiltrer` (covert, a person, **your word**) · `extraire` (hostage
rescue, medical/tactical, **and a judicial term** for moving a prisoner — risk of
wrong reading) · `évacuer` (overt, official) · **`récupérer`** (informal spoken —
« On va le récupérer. »).

## Compromise

**BURNED → `brûlé`** (classic, institutional) and **`grillé`** (familiar,
spoken) — near-synonyms. **`cramé`** is the most familiar. Wiktionnaire even
preserves the escalating chain: « nous sommes cuits, brûlés, grillés,
carbonisés. »
`brûler`/`griller quelqu'un` · **`se faire griller`** (most natural spoken form)
· `griller une source` · `un agent brûlé`.
**Trap: there is no French *burn notice*.** Paraphrase: « il a été désavoué » ·
« on l'a lâché » · « il est brûlé, le service ne le reconnaît plus ». Do not
invent « une notice de brûlure ».

**COUNTER-INTELLIGENCE → `le contre-espionnage`** (classic, universally
understood, abbreviated `le CE`) and **`la contre-ingérence`** (the current
administrative term of art). **`L'ingérence` is currently the most fashionable
word in French security discourse** — if the setting is now, use it.
`la lutte antiterroriste` (**`la LAT`**) is the actual French institutional
phrasing; « contre-terrorisme » is slightly anglicising.
Period markers: **SDECE** → DGSE (1982); **DST** → DCRI (2008) → **DGSI** (2014).
Getting the era's service name right is a cheap authenticity win.

**DEBRIEF → `le débriefing`**, verb **`débriefer`** — English root kept and
conjugated. In-service French also says **`un compte rendu`** / **`un CR`** —
« Tu me feras un CR. » is more authentic than « débriefing » for the document.
**fr-FR vs Québec:** France uses `débriefing`; Québécois `débreffage` would be a
tell.

## Hard cases

**HONEYPOT → `un piège à miel`**, also **`un piège amoureux`**. Both live in
2024–26 French journalism, both usually in scare quotes.
**Caution:** `un honeypot` in French *tech* writing means the cybersecurity
decoy server. Keep them apart.
*(Unverified: `une hirondelle` / `un corbeau` for seductress/seducer agents are
widely repeated in French spy literature but could not be confirmed. Check
against Poirot & Moutouh, Dictionnaire du renseignement, Perrin 2018.)*

**BLACK SITE → `une prison secrète`** (the press default, overwhelmingly) or
`un site noir` (calque, NGO/human-rights register, used with guillemets).
Use the former in narration; the latter only when a character is quoting
Anglo-American jargon.

**PLAUSIBLE DENIABILITY → `le déni plausible`** exists but is **essentially
absent from live French journalism** — it reads as a translated technical term.
What French actually writes: « l'État doit pouvoir démentir » ·
« ne pas être traçable jusqu'à nous » · « l'opération ne doit pas être
attribuable » · `la non-attribution`.
**For a game:** put `déni plausible` in a briefing document or a bureaucrat's
mouth, where its stiffness is characterful — and use
**« Si ça tourne mal, on ne vous connaît pas. »** in dialogue. That is the real
French of it.

**TRADECRAFT → no French noun exists. Do not calque.**
Use **`le métier`** — « C'est le métier qui rentre. » / « Il connaît le
métier. » — the closest single-word equivalent and what professionals say. Also
**`les savoir-faire`** (the DGSE's own *Clandestinité* entry uses exactly this,
effectively defining tradecraft without a word for it) · `les modes
opératoires` · `les techniques opérationnelles`.
For "sloppy tradecraft": « du travail d'amateur » · « ça sent l'amateurisme ».
**Never « le tradecraft ».**
Real French tradecraft terms to use instead, all DGSE-attested:
**`le désilhouettage`** (changing appearance to blend in — verb
`se désilhouetter`) · `l'itinéraire de sécurité` · `le cloisonnement` ·
`le besoin d'en connaître` · `le tamponnage` · `faire un environnement`. And
from DGSE training slang: « **On a des stages de clochardisation** » — training
in passing as homeless.

**RECRUITMENT LEVERS → `les leviers`.** The DGSE explicitly calls MICE
« l'acronyme **anglais** », so a French officer citing MICE is knowingly quoting
English. French Wikipedia records a francised version: **`VICE`** = Vénal,
Idéologie, Compromission, Ego. **Delightful, genuinely French, almost unknown.**

| Term | Who says it | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **la Piscine** | **Media/outsiders only** | From the neighbouring swimming pool on bd Mortier. **An officer saying it is a tell that they are not really inside.** |
| **la Boîte** | **Insiders** | The in-house word. Same warm-cynical tone as "the Agency". |
| **la Crèmerie** | **Insiders**, jocular | Wonderfully odd, very authentic, low outside recognition. |
| **la Centrale** / `la portion centrale` | Insiders | |
| **Mortier** | Insiders | Metonymy. « Ça vient de Mortier. » |
| **la Maison** | Broad French institutional usage | |
| **le service** / **les services** | Everyone | Singular = my service. Plural = the community, faintly sinister in press. |
| **Levallois** | Press/insiders, DGSI | HQ metonym — **moving to Saint-Ouen ~2028**, so it has a shelf life. |

**`Une barbouze`** — **feminine even for a man**, argot, **strongly pejorative**
and politically loaded (Gaullist irregulars against the OAS in Algeria; then any
SDECE operative). Connotes deniable dirty work and Françafrique. Derived:
**`une barbouzerie`**, still alive in the press. **Use it for a hostile
character's description of your protagonist, or a private-sector operator —
never as the neutral narrator's word for a DGSE officer.**

## What French readers expect from thriller prose

1. **Restraint, not adrenaline.** *Le Bureau des Légendes*' creator worked from
   « les valeurs de la DGSE — sérieux et rigueur », and the show deliberately
   avoids a « lexique rocambolesque à la Q dans James Bond ». Gadget-porn and
   wisecracks read as American.
2. **Jargon used flatly, without explanation — that is the pleasure.** A former
   DGSE analyst reviewing the series: « Il y a plein de formules et de
   raisonnements qui sont propres au service. » **Do not gloss `BLM`, `OT`, `HC`
   or `légende` in-line.** His one complaint was *invented vulgarity* — a
   coinage he'd never heard in the service. **French audiences forgive obscure
   jargon but notice fake slang instantly.**
3. **Vouvoiement is a plot device.** An `officier traitant` and their `source`
   will very often stay on `vous` for years — professional distance is doctrine.
   A handler switching to `tu` is a manipulation move; a source switching to
   `tu` is either intimacy or a threat. **English localisation files have no
   slot for this. Decide it deliberately or the French will feel flat.**
4. **Register split by character type.** `flic` vs `policier` vs `fonctionnaire
   de police`; `indic` vs `source`; `planque` vs `dispositif`; `rencard` vs
   `rendez-vous`; `opé` vs `opération`; `grillé` vs `brûlé`. **A DGSE officer
   and a BAC cop must not sound alike.**
5. **Rhythm.** French commercial thrillers use short verbless sentences, present
   tense for action, and **passé composé rather than passé simple** (passé
   simple signals literary/period register). Avoid stacked adjectives; French
   prefers noun + prepositional phrase.
6. **Keep only the English words French readers actually keep:** `IMSI-catcher`,
   `débriefing`, `HUMINT`/`SIGINT`/`OSINT`, `honeypot` (cyber sense only),
   `briefing`, `check-point`. **Not kept, and a tell if used:** `intel`,
   `asset`, `tradecraft`, `safe house`, `burn notice`, `handler`, `stakeout`,
   `dead drop`, `black site`, `chatter`.
7. **Québec-isms to avoid:** `contrôleur de sources`/`contrôleur d'agents`,
   `débreffage`, over-francised compounds. When in doubt, France keeps the
   loanword.
