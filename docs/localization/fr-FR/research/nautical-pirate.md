# Nautical / pirate French (fr-FR) — Blackwake register

Sources include the full text of Théo Varlet's canonical French *L'Île au
trésor* (grepped in full, so every quotation is verbatim), a glossary of French
sea-shanty vocabulary compiled from attested lyrics, fr.wikipedia/wiktionary,
fr.wikiquote for the *Pirates des Caraïbes* VF, and French maritime-history
sites.

## The register ladder — read this first

Four words for "ship" are not synonyms.

| Word | Gender | Register | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **bateau** | m | everyday, neutral-familiar | safe everywhere, slightly civilian |
| **navire** | m | standard-formal, default written word | **the workhorse** |
| **bâtiment** | m | Marine Nationale / naval-professional | warships and naval officers only — never a merchantman |
| **vaisseau** | m | historical / emphatic / literary | perfect for age of sail (`vaisseau de ligne`); dead for modern ships |

Rule: narration = `navire`; pirate dialogue = `navire`/`bateau`; naval officers
and the Crown = `bâtiment`/`vaisseau`; the player's own ship affectionately =
`le rafiot`.

## Ship parts

Hull `la coque` · deck `le pont` · keel `la quille` · hold `la cale`
(`à fond de cale` = down in the hold, the idiom for imprisoning someone) ·
bilge `la sentine` (pejorative, "cesspit") · gunwale `le bastingage` ·
scuppers `les dalots` · companionway `la coursive` · hatch `l'écoutille` ·
gun port `le sabord` (verb `saborder` = to scuttle) · storeroom `la cambuse`.

**Bow/stern register trap.** `l'étrave` = the structural stem, technical.
`la proue` = the bow as a whole, literary (survives in `figure de proue`).
`l'avant` = the plain word sailors use. `le taille-mer` = cutwater.
`le beaupré` = bowsprit. Do not use `étrave` for the general bow —
"L'ennemi approche par l'avant", not "par l'étrave".
`la poupe` = stern, literary (alive in `avoir le vent en poupe`).
`l'arrière` = neutral. `le tableau arrière` = transom. `l'étambot` = sternpost.

**Port/starboard.** `bâbord` = port, `tribord` = starboard. Both universally
known. `le navire partit bâbord amures` (Varlet) — `amure` = the tack.

**Steering — important trap.** `le gouvernail` = the rudder blade.
**`la barre` = the helm, and this is the one you want**: `prendre la barre`,
`à la barre`, `l'homme de barre`. `prendre le gouvernail` is what a non-sailor
writes. `le timonier` = helmsman (period/naval); `le barreur` = modern yachting.

**Masts and rigging.** `le mât de misaine` (fore) → `le grand mât` (main) →
`le mât d'artimon` (mizzen). Sections: `le bas-mât` → `le mât de hune` →
`le mât de perroquet` → `le mât de cacatois`. `la vergue` = yard;
**`à bout de vergue`** = at the yardarm, and Varlet uses it for hangings.
`le gréement` = rigging. `le hauban` = shroud. `la drisse` = halyard.
`l'écoute` = sheet. `la garcette` = short line, **and historically the rope's
end used for flogging** — excellent for a disciplinarian.

**The `corde` shibboleth.** The generic term is `le cordage`. Sailors' lore
holds there is no `corde` aboard, only `cordages`, `manœuvres` and `bouts`.
Treat as strong convention rather than a verified rule. **Never have a sailor
say `corde`** — but a landlubber saying it and a sailor correcting him is a
free authenticity beat.

**Sails.** Main mast bottom-to-top: `la grand-voile` → `le grand hunier` →
`le grand perroquet` → `le grand cacatois`. Fore: `la misaine` →
`le petit hunier` → `le petit perroquet` → `le petit cacatois`.
`la brigantine` = spanker · `le foc` = jib · `la trinquette` = staysail ·
`la bonnette` = studding sail · `la civadière` = spritsail.
**`le perroquet` is a false friend — it also means "parrot."** Varlet plays on
it deliberately.

**Crow's nest trap.** `la hune` = the authentic age-of-sail platform.
`la vigie` = the lookout, **feminine even for a man** (`la vigie a crié`).
**`le nid-de-pie` is a calque** and belongs to later whalers — avoid it.

**Raised decks.** `le gaillard d'avant` = forecastle (crew).
`le gaillard d'arrière` = quarterdeck (officers, the wheel).
`la dunette` = poop deck, a structure *on top of* the quarterdeck.
**Trap: `dunette` ≠ quarterdeck.** Translating it so moves your captain up a deck.

**Gangplank.** `la passerelle` in modern French means the ship's **bridge**.
The boarding opening is **`la coupée`**; the ladder `l'échelle de coupée`.

**brig vs brick — confirmed false friend.** `le brick` = the two-masted ship
type. English "the brig" = ship's prison has **no connection**. Use
**`les fers`** (`mettre aux fers` — most idiomatic and atmospheric),
`le cachot`, `aux arrêts`, or `à fond de cale`. Never `au brick`.

## Crew roles

**The quartermaster trap — the biggest landmine.** In the modern French Navy
`quartier-maître` is the **lowest rank above ordinary seaman**. In pirate
context French historiography nonetheless uses it as the standard term, and
Varlet uses it for Long John Silver. So: the word is defensible and canonical,
**but a French player with navy exposure reads "junior enlisted man."**
Recommendation: use it, but establish the role explicitly in-fiction the first
time — `le quartier-maître, second après le capitaine et élu par l'équipage`.
Use **`le second`** where you need unambiguous seniority.

captain `le capitaine` · first mate **`le second`** (not `le lieutenant`, which
is a rank, not a role) · boatswain `le maître d'équipage`, short form
**`le bosco`** (technical maritime, not slang — punchy and authentic) ·
gunner `le canonnier` · carpenter `le charpentier` · surgeon
`le chirurgien de bord` · sailmaker **`le maître voilier`** (bare `le voilier`
means a sailing ship) · cook **`le coq`** / `le maître coq` (the authentic word;
`le cuisinier` is modern-flat) · topman `le gabier` (specified by mast:
`le gabier d'misaine`) · sailor `le matelot` · ship's boy `le mousse`
(`le moussaillon` is cutesy and Haddock-coded) · lookout `la vigie` ·
master-at-arms `le capitaine d'armes`, slang `le bidel` ·
shipowner `l'armateur`.

**Powder monkey has no established French term.** The role was done by
`mousses`. Use `le mousse` or paraphrase — do not coin a compound.

## Actions and commands

**Getting under way.** `appareiller` is the correct technical verb.
`mettre à la voile` is historical and correct.
**`mettre les voiles` is a MAJOR TRAP** — in modern French it is colloquial for
"to clear off / do a runner." Using it for "set sail" reads as a pun.
`lever l'ancre` · `mouiller` (to anchor) · `jeter l'ancre` ·
`larguer les amarres` · `prendre le large` · `mettre le cap sur`.

**Sail handling.** `hisser` · `ferler` (furl) · `carguer` (clew up) ·
`prendre un ris` (reef) · **`larguer les ris`** = *shake out* reefs, the
opposite · `border` (sheet in) / `choquer` (ease) · `haler` ·
**`mettre en panne`** = to heave to (confirmed: pirates hoisted the black flag
"pour inviter leur cible à se mettre en panne").

**Tacking.** `virer de bord` · `empanner` (gybe) · `le lof`.
**The real command protocol, still used on French boats today:**
« Paré à virer ? » → « Paré ! » → « Envoyez ! » — an authentic call-and-response
worth using as an interactive beat.

**Combat.** `aborder` / `l'abordage` · **« À l'abordage ! »** (genuine,
universal, not comic — use freely) · `le grappin d'abordage` ·
`le sabre d'abordage` · `la bordée` (broadside; note `tirer une bordée` in
sailor slang means a run ashore) · **`le branle-bas de combat`** (genuine and
still a live idiom) · `sans quartier` · `baisser pavillon` (surrender).

## Ship types

`la frégate` · `le sloop` · `le brick` · `le brigantin` · `le galion` ·
**`la goélette`** (schooner — this is the Hispaniola in French) · `la corvette` ·
`la galère` (careful: `c'est la galère` is very common modern slang) ·
`le vaisseau de ligne` · `le chébec` (Barbary corsairs) · `la flûte` ·
`la caravelle` · `le trois-mâts` · `le ponton` (dismasted hulk).

## pirate / corsaire / flibustier / boucanier / forban — NOT synonyms

French readers care about this a great deal.

- **`le pirate`** — sea bandit on his own account, no commission. Neutral default.
- **`le corsaire`** — holds a `lettre de marque`, legal, POW status if caught.
  **Culturally huge in France** — corsairs are national heroes. Never a mere
  synonym for pirate.
- **`le flibustier`** — Caribbean, 17th–18th c. **This is the word Varlet uses
  throughout for Flint's men.** Strongly recommended for a Golden-Age setting.
- **`le boucanier`** — originally hunters who smoke-cured meat. **Not a synonym
  for pirate**; using it as one is a visible error.
- **`le forban`** — raiding for personal profit without a commission. Warmer,
  more roguish, literary. There is a famous shanty called *Le Forban*.
- **`l'écumeur des mers`** — elevated, dated, literary. Right for a wanted
  poster or a proclamation; wrong for casual dialogue.
- **`les Frères de la Côte`** — the Tortuga confederation. Faction-name ready.

**A gift from Varlet:** `les gentilshommes de fortune` — period-perfect, ironic,
and canonical.

## Privateering, flags, mutiny

`la lettre de marque` · **`la course`** = the French word for privateering
itself (`la guerre de course`, `armer en course`) — far more authentic than any
calque · `la prise` · `le butin`.

**`le pavillon`, never `le drapeau`, at sea.** `le pavillon noir` = the Jolly
Roger, hoisted to invite surrender. **`le pavillon rouge` = no quarter** — a
great escalation beat. `Jolly Roger` is **kept untranslated** in French,
including in Varlet, with a folk etymology from `joli rouge`.

`la mutinerie` / `se mutiner` / `le mutin` — all attested in Varlet, no traps.

**Marooning:** avoid `marronnage` (that is the escape of enslaved people, and
carries that weight). Varlet's solution is better: `le marron` for the abandoned
man, and `abandonner sur une île déserte` for the act.

**Walk the plank: French has no native idiom.** Use `la planche` for the object
and **« À la planche ! »** (parallel to « À l'abordage ! ») or
« Fais-le passer par-dessus bord ! ». Avoid `marcher sur la planche` — flat
translationese. It does not appear in Varlet at all.

## Oaths — authentic vs pastiche

**Tier 1, safe:** « À l'abordage ! » · « Ohé ! » · « Paré à virer ! » ·
« Hisse et ho ! » · « Branle-bas de combat ! » ·
**« Mille tonnerres ! »** (attested in Varlet — the top recommendation for a
signature oath) · « Par tous les diables ! » · « Sans quartier ! »

**Tier 2, hijacked — use only with intent:**
**« Mille sabords ! »** is authentic in origin but **100% Capitaine Haddock**
in France today (100+ uses across Tintin). A French player hears Tintin, not a
pirate. Same for **« Tonnerre de Brest ! »** and « Marin d'eau douce ! ».

**Tier 3:** « Hissez haut ! » is the refrain of Hugues Aufray's *Santiano* —
every French person knows it, but its colour is campfire/childhood, not danger.

**Tier 4, AVOID ENTIRELY: « Sacrebleu ! »** It is essentially dead in living
French and survives only as the **anglophone stereotype of a Frenchman**. A
French player seeing it will immediately read it as translated-by-a-non-speaker.
**Probably the single most damaging item available.** Same family: morbleu,
palsambleu, ventrebleu, parbleu, corbleu. Also avoid « Nom d'une pipe ! »,
« Sapristi ! », « Saperlipopette ! ».

## Quebec leakage to keep OUT

`embarquer`/`débarquer` for cars (fr-FR: `monter`/`descendre`) ·
`virer de bord` figuratively (fr-FR: nautical only) · `bordée de neige` ·
`manquer le bateau` (fr-FR: `rater le coche`) · `couler un examen` ·
`traversier` (fr-FR: `ferry`/`transbordeur`).

## Recommendations

1. Default ship noun: **`le navire`**.
2. Default word for the pirates: **`flibustiers`** (Varlet's choice).
3. Signature oath: **« Mille tonnerres ! »** — avoid `mille sabords`.
4. **Never ship `Sacrebleu`.**
5. Fix these five before anything else: brig→`les fers` · set sail→`appareiller` ·
   quarterdeck→`gaillard d'arrière` · take the helm→`prendre la barre` ·
   sailmaker→`maître voilier`.
6. **Choose a register consciously.** Two coherent options: the Varlet/Hornblower
   register (technical — `coq`, `gabier`, `bosco`, `hune`, `dalots`) or the One
   Piece register (modern — `cuisinier`, `charpentier`, `navigatrice`).
   One Piece FR uses plain modern job nouns throughout and is **not** a source of
   authentic nautical French — but it is what an anime audience expects. A
   **One Piece base with Varlet garnish** — plain job titles, authentic ship
   parts and commands — is probably the sweet spot.
7. **Do not domesticate iconic proper nouns.** Varlet kept `Jolly Roger` and
   `Yo-ho-ho`; Glénat was publicly burned for over-translating One Piece names
   (Katakuri → "Dent-de-chien"). French genre readers raised on fan translations
   expect names to survive.
